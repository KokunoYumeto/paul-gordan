\end{aligned}

This line, since it is shorter than neighboring lines, shall be called shortest.

And, lest perhaps an error be introduced by this name, let us call that line, than which no shorter line at all can be conceived, the shortest of all; this is to be understood as a part of the shortest line, while this part itself is a part of the geodetic.

\begin{center}\S\ 2.\end{center}

Since the integral
\[
 \int_{l_1}^{l_2}\left(
{dl\,\delta\!\bigl({d\psi\over dl}\bigr)\delta\!\bigl({d\psi\over dl}\bigr)\cos^2l[1-e^2\cos^2l]
\over \{1-e^2\cos^2l+\cos^2l\,{d\psi\over dl}\cdot{d\psi\over dl}\}^{1\frac12}}
\right)
\]
and each element of it are known to be positive, the whole integral must be positive. It would seem to agree with this that every geodetic line, even in its entire extension, is shortest.

This is not so, because in Chapter II, \S\ 7, we showed that the force of our equation holds only up to the point at which the circle \gq{$-l_0$} is touched by the geodetic line.

\begin{center}\S\ 3.\end{center}

Therefore, since from the integral equation which we proposed above one cannot know how far a geodetic line, even after touching the circle \gq{$-l_0$}, is shortest, universal rules must be established. We shall obtain this by introducing, in place of the variable of integration $l$, another variable of integration, $\psi$, which evidently increases perpetually in proportion as the point $\Pi$ recedes from the origin.
